# Tone guide

The Modern American Trail is satire. A party escapes a liberal paradise (Portland), drives through a country run by an authoritarian regime, stops at other liberal paradises along the way, and tries to reach the ultimate one: Vermont. That framing is deliberate and stays.

## The voice

**Dry, specific, absurd but plausible.** The best jokes are a real-world detail pushed one step further than it would go: a radar gun with a coin slot, a loyalty oath with an arbitration clause, a beagle trained on paperbacks specifically. If a line would work in any game, it isn't specific enough.

- **Understate.** Never wink. The narrator reports absurdity in a flat, matter-of-fact voice and moves on.
- **Concrete nouns beat adjectives.** "A flag made of corporate logos" beats "a ridiculous flag."
- **Short.** Event text is two or three sentences. Outcomes are one or two.
- **Let the reader do the last step.** "The pledge has a promo code in it" is funnier than explaining why that's bad.
- **Earned warmth.** Kindness shows up on the road too: a woman who whispers "Godspeed," a teacher who feeds you lasagna. The contrast is what makes the satire land.

## What the jokes are aimed at

**The regime and its machinery:** checkpoints, loyalty tests, surveillance, sponsored patriotism, book bans, heritage inspections, megachurch toll plazas, militia roadblocks, coin-operated justice, slogans, paperwork, and the people enforcing them *in their role as enforcers*.

**Systems, institutions, and behavior,** not groups of people. A deputy running a speed trap is fair game; "people from Kentucky" is not. A megachurch charging a tithe at a bridge is fair game; believers are not. Rural characters, church members, and working people appear often, and they're frequently the ones who help. The pastor who got defrocked for being too nice, the out-of-work miner who tells you the truth about the pensions, the church lady with the biscuits: these are the hellhole's best people, and the regime is what they're living under.

**The paradises get affectionate ribbing.** The co-op with a 40-step membership process, the land acknowledgment that runs long, the protest about a font, the bike lane that eats your afternoon. The home team isn't spotless, and the jokes land harder because of it. The paradises are still the safe havens: help is real there, and the ribbing never undercuts that.

## Hard lines

- No jokes whose punchline is a race, ethnicity, religion, disability, gender identity or sexual orientation.
- No real, named public figures. The regime is the governor, the deputy, the sponsor: archetypes, not people.
- No slurs, including "ironic" ones.
- Violence is offscreen and consequences are stated plainly. No gore.
- Real-world tragedies are not material.

## Mechanics in the text

- Every choice should be understandable before you pick it. Risky choices *sound* risky.
- Outcomes should explain their effects in the fiction: if food drops, say what happened to it.
- Use the tokens: `{member}` (someone in the party), `{skilled}` (whoever is making the skill check), `{leader}`, `{stop}`.
- Skill-check successes should make the skilled character look competent in a specific way. Failures should be funny, not humiliating.

## Checklist for a new event

1. Is there a specific, concrete image in the first sentence?
2. Is the target a system or institution, not a group of people?
3. Do the choices mean different things, not three flavors of the same thing?
4. Is there at least one choice anyone can take (no skill, item or money required)?
5. Would it still make sense in any region it's tagged for?
6. Does `npm run validate` pass?
